"Double-entry" sounds like something only accountants need to understand. It is not. The idea is simple, it is more than 500 years old, and once you see it with a real sale from your own shop, the reports your accountant gives you start to make sense.

This article explains double-entry accounting in plain words, walks through one credit sale step by step, and shows why it matters even if you never type a journal yourself.

## The one idea behind it

Every transaction has two sides. Money or value always comes **from** somewhere and goes **to** somewhere.

- When you sell goods for cash, stock leaves the shelf and cash comes into the drawer.
- When you buy stock on credit, stock arrives and you now owe the supplier.
- When you pay a salary, cash leaves an account and the business has a salary expense.

Double-entry accounting records both sides of every transaction. Single-entry, like a simple sales book, records only one side. That is why a sales book can tell you how much you sold, but not how much profit you made or what your business is worth.

## Accounts, debits and credits

In double-entry, every place money can sit or come from is an **account**: Cash, Zaad Merchant, Inventory, Accounts receivable (what customers owe you), Sales revenue, Cost of goods sold, Salaries, and so on. The full list is your **chart of accounts**.

Each transaction is written as a **journal**: one or more accounts are **debited** and one or more are **credited**, and the total debits always equal the total credits.

Debit and credit are just the names of the two columns. They do not mean good or bad. As a rough guide:

| Type of account | Goes up with a | Goes down with a |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assets (cash, stock, money owed to you) | Debit | Credit |
| Expenses (cost of goods, rent, salaries) | Debit | Credit |
| Liabilities (money you owe) | Credit | Debit |
| Income (sales) | Credit | Debit |

You do not need to memorise this. What matters is the rule: **debits equal credits in every journal.**

## A real sale, step by step

Let us follow one sale at Hargeisa Spare Parts, the sample business we use throughout MaamulPlus.

Abdi Ahmed, a regular customer, buys parts worth **$950.00** on credit. The parts cost the shop **$612.40** when they were bought.

Two things happened:

1. **The sale itself.** Abdi now owes the shop $950.00, and the shop has earned $950.00 of sales.
2. **The goods left the shelf.** Stock worth $612.40 at cost is gone, and that is the cost of making this sale.

The journal looks like this:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `1200` Accounts receivable | $950.00 | |
| `4000` Sales revenue | | $950.00 |
| `5000` Cost of goods sold | $612.40 | |
| `1300` Inventory | | $612.40 |
| **Total** | **$1,562.40** | **$1,562.40** |

Debits equal credits. The journal is balanced.

Read it line by line:

- **Accounts receivable goes up by $950.00.** Abdi owes you money; that is an asset.
- **Sales revenue goes up by $950.00.** You made a sale.
- **Cost of goods sold goes up by $612.40.** That is what the sale cost you.
- **Inventory goes down by $612.40.** The parts are no longer on your shelf.

From this one journal you already know the gross profit on the sale: $950.00 minus $612.40, or **$337.60**.

## When Abdi pays

A week later, Abdi pays $430.00 by Zaad. The journal:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `1041` Zaad Merchant | $430.00 | |
| `1200` Accounts receivable | | $430.00 |

Money arrived in the Zaad wallet, and Abdi owes $430.00 less. Notice there is no sales revenue here: the sale was already recorded when the invoice was written. A payment only moves money from "owed to us" to "in our account".

This is exactly why the sales list in MaamulPlus shows INV-000214 as **partly paid**, with a balance of $520.00.

## The trial balance

If every journal balances, then the totals of all debits and all credits across all accounts must also be equal. A list of every account with its debit or credit balance is called the **trial balance**.

When the trial balance does not balance, something was recorded on only one side. With a notebook or spreadsheet, finding the mistake can take days. With a system that only accepts balanced journals, it cannot happen in the first place.

## From journals to statements

Once every transaction is recorded with both sides, the main financial statements come straight out of the accounts:

- **Income statement:** income accounts minus expense accounts for a period. Did the business make a profit this month?
- **Balance sheet:** asset accounts, liability accounts and the owner's equity at a point in time. What does the business own and owe?
- **Cash flow:** movements in the cash, bank and mobile money accounts. Where did the money come from and where did it go?

Hargeisa Spare Parts can see, for example, that it holds $7,436.03 across its cash, bank and mobile money accounts, that customers owe $1,284.36, and that stock on the shelves is worth $17,979.10 at cost, all from the same books.
